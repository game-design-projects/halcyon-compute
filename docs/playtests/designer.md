## 2. Grade vs Lecture 3 (characteristics / depth)

**Characteristics actually exhibited in play:**
- **Time granularity — pausable real time, confirmed.** Space pauses, 1/2/3/4-key speeds all worked exactly as documented. I played almost entirely fast-forwarded and paused only for chapter modals; this is a legitimate strategy-not-dexterity mode, matching the lecture's category.
- **Stochasticity — low and at the "whole game" level, confirmed.** Across both runs on the same seed, the same news beats recurred in the same order (Lattice's fate, HBM scares, heat waves) — evidence the randomness is a seed-level draw, not per-action dice, exactly as DESIGN.md claims.
- **Observability — imperfect and biased, confirmed but under-felt.** I saw vendor/HBM "scare" news items and the eventual "behind the curtain" reveal that some were false, but because I never opened a pilot card or read the memory-market drawer, I experienced this system only passively, as flavor text, not as a decision I was making. The mechanic is real; I didn't feel the read-the-signals loop the lecture cares about, because nothing forced me to act on a signal.
- **Systems (conditional/combination/feedback/resource), confirmed, and this is the game's strongest area.** I *directly experienced* a conditional system (rack throttles/earns nothing without transit — see §6, Bug 1), a resource crunch (cash, power, network, technicians all bound me at different points), and a feedback loop (failures compounding into bankruptcy in Run 1, auto-repair breaking that loop in Run 2).
- **Depth vs entropy — the score gap is the real evidence, and it's large.** $257k (my careful-but-not-optimizing play) vs $15.61M (Greedy, a dumb "buy whatever pays today" heuristic bot) vs $142.28M (Planner). Even *Greedy* beat me by 60×, and I was not idle — I built racks, enabled auto-repair, and finished all 17 chapters without going bankrupt. That is a strong, first-hand confirmation of "the more you think, the better you do": doing the bare-minimum obvious things (sign contracts, watch the roofline, size the grid) that I skipped is apparently worth orders of magnitude. This matches DESIGN.md/SPEC.md's own bot-ablation table (Full game: Planner ~701% ahead of Greedy, n=12 seeds — consistent with what I felt at n=1).

**Heuristics a player plausibly learns from play (not just from reading DESIGN.md):**
1. "Watch the cash chip's red border" — learned this the hard way in Run 1; effective, unambiguous signal.
2. "Turn on auto-repair the moment it's offered" — directly reversed my cash trajectory in Run 2. Good, teachable, single-click heuristic.
3. "New racks are not free money the day you place them" — learned only through the bankruptcy postmortem, not through play (see Bug 1). This is a heuristic the *design intends* to teach (SPEC.md heuristic list item 1, "find the binding constraint") but that my play never surfaced correctly, because the actual binding constraint (transit) wasn't visible as a named thing until three chapters after it started binding.
4. **Heuristics I never learned because nothing pushed me to engage them**: roofline card-matching, contract-signing as a launch hedge, pilot-before-scale. DESIGN.md's own heuristic list (9 items) requires touching systems (contracts, pilots, memory market) that passive play never has to touch to avoid losing. This is a genuine depth-vs-onboarding gap: the systems that carry the most measured depth (DESIGN.md's own ablation table ranks **contracts as the single biggest depth contributor**, "removing them roughly halves the planner's score") are exactly the ones a first-time, cautious player is least likely to open, because the game never makes not-opening-them costly until the final scorecard.

**Where depth is claimed but not felt:** the design docs claim rich depth in memory-market timing, policy lobbying, and investor dilution tradeoffs; in an actual sitting, none of these ever forced a decision on me — I could (and did) coast through all three without consequence up to the very end, and the HUD gave no running comparison to an "expected" score, so I had no mid-game signal that I was leaving 99.8% of the score on the table. Depth that only resolves at the final screen is depth a player can't act on.

## 4. Scope & onboarding

- **17 chapters is too many for how they're paced.** Empirically: Chapters 1–4 land inside the first ~120 of 1800 days (~7% of playtime) — four new systems (racks/switches, power/eco-modes, GPU/roofline, seasonal heat) inside the first "quarter" of the game, while the player is still learning the drag-to-install interaction. Chapter 17 (the last new mechanic) doesn't land until day ~1310 — **the final 27% of the campaign (490 days) introduces nothing new.** The pacing curve is front-loaded far more than the day-schedule in SPEC.md (`d0, d30, d60, d120, d240, d300, d420…`) might suggest at a glance; in practice, feeling four unrelated systems (currency, capacity, market-matching, thermal planning) arrive in the first month of play is a lot for a player who hasn't yet lost a game and doesn't know what matters.
- **Cut/merge candidates:** Chapters 9 (Memory market), 16 (Policy), and to a lesser extent 12 (Energy sourcing) are the ones I could ignore for 3+ years with zero consequence in this run — they never forced a decision, matching DESIGN.md's own ablation numbers (memory market and policy rank near the bottom of measured depth contribution, "+592…+811%," vs contracts at "+387%," i.e., removing them barely narrows the greedy/planner gap). These three are good candidates to merge into fewer, more consequential chapters, or to gate their *first* event behind an actual required decision rather than a modal.
- **Chapter pacing vs consequence is inverted.** The chapters most likely to bankrupt a careless player (2/3, because of the transit trap — see Bug 1) arrive earliest, before the player has any tools (auto-repair, spares shelf, contracts) to recover. The chapters that could use urgency to teach depth (8: contracts, 14: investors) arrive mid-to-late, once the player has already learned to coast.

## 5. Slop check

- **Not slop:** the systems interlock in ways that feel authored, not generated — the roofline formula (`throughput = min(compute, bandwidth × intensity)`), the seed-consistent narrative payoff (Lattice hype→shutdown foreshadowed and paid off across two independent playthroughs of the same seed), and the postmortem's specificity ("you lost $23k to failed hardware downtime, most of it in Y3 winter") all read as deliberate, human-designed content — an LLM's "model of players" tends to produce generic praise/failure text, not seed-anchored, mechanically-derived specifics like these.
- **Where it needs a human designer's decision:** the pacing mismatch above (§4) and the transit trap (§6, Bug 1) are exactly the kind of thing that reads as *unreviewed* rather than *unintentional* — the individual systems are well thought through, but nobody appears to have played chapters 1–7 back-to-back as a first-timer and watched a rack sit dead for a year. That's not an LLM-slop failure (the systems are too specific and interlocking for that), it's a **missing playtest pass** failure — the exact gap this report exists to fill.
- **The end-screen bot comparison is the single best anti-slop decision in the game.** Comparing the human to a dumb heuristic bot and a lookahead bot, on the *same seed*, is a legitimately rigorous way to let a player calibrate "was that good," rather than an LLM-flavored "Great job, Founder!" platitude.

## 6. Claims vs experience (read after playing)

- **DESIGN.md is stale and self-contradictory as a top-level doc.** Its opening table describes a "3 years (1080 days)... 6 short chapters" game, but its own bot-ablation section three pages later references "the planner... across all 17 chapters" and an ablation table with 16 named chapter systems (facilities, energy, environment, investors, reputation, policy, disruption, etc.) that only exist in the 17-chapter, 5-year game I actually played. `docs/SPEC.md` clarifies this is intentional — DESIGN.md is explicitly labeled the frozen "v1" week-3 prototype doc and SPEC.md is "v2," the current contract — but nothing in DESIGN.md itself flags that it's superseded. A reader who opens DESIGN.md first (as this brief instructed) gets actively wrong scope expectations for ~2 more pages before the numbers stop making sense.
- **SPEC.md's own invariant is violated in play.** §0: *"Mechanics are inactive until their chapter (no hidden drain before the player is told)."* My Run 1 directly falsifies this for M28 (internet transit, Ch.7/M28): racks I built in Chapter 1–3 needed transit capacity from the moment they went live, showed a persistent, unexplained "Network short: 0 of N needed" status, and earned $0 for **8+ real months of game time**, before Chapter 7 ever told me what "transit" was — a textbook hidden drain before the player is told, which the game's own design contract says should not happen. This is the single most consequential mismatch between claim and experience in the whole review; it's also the proximate cause of Run 1's bankruptcy.
- **GAME_FEEL.md's verifiable claims mostly held up** (see §3 table) — floating money, undo toast, red-bordered cash gauge, chapter confetti, and the quarter-close ledger toast all matched their described behavior closely. The claims I couldn't verify (screen shake, ticking counters, ambient audio/hum) are unconfirmed, not contradicted, given this playtest's fast-forward/screenshot method.
- **Balance target claim, partially checked:** SPEC.md §6 claims "Idle player survives but ends < 5% of the planner score." My Run 2 was not idle — I built hardware, enabled auto-repair, finished all chapters without bankruptcy — yet still scored **0.18%** of the planner's total, well under even the stated *idle* threshold. If a moderately-engaged player scores in the same tier as the design's own "idle" baseline, the mid-game feedback gap in §2 (no running comparison to "on pace") looks like the real culprit, not the depth model itself.

## 7. Bugs

1. **(Major) Silent pre-chapter resource dependency ("network short" trap).** Repro: New campaign, seed 303. In Chapter 1–3 (real days 0–60), place any web-server or GPU rack beyond the two starting racks, with a switch present and installed. The rack's panel shows "Network short: 0 of N needed" and $0/d revenue indefinitely. This does not resolve on its own; it only starts resolving after Chapter 7 (day ~420) introduces "internet transit" as a purchasable global resource. Racks placed this early can sit dead-revenue for 8+ months while still drawing power/upkeep. Directly caused Run 1's bankruptcy (racks A4/A5/B1 never produced revenue from install to game-over). Screenshots: `07_ch2.png` → `24_a3panel.png` (same rack, same "short" status, 7 months apart in game time).
2. **(Minor, n=3) Sell-via-drag did not work.** Three attempts to drag an installed rack (from the floor tile, and from the rack's detail-panel visual) onto the "Drop hardware here to sell" zone did not sell the hardware in any attempt (`27_sellA4.png`–`29_sell3.png`). Could be user/driver-coordinate error rather than a real bug — flagging as unconfirmed with low confidence (n=3, same method each time, so may be one systematic mistake rather than three independent failures).
3. **(Minor) Transitional score value flash.** On the final "Five years are up" screen, an early poll of `/text` returned `SCORE … $31k` while the rendered modal (screenshotted moments later) showed the settled `$257k`. Likely a one-frame stale DOM read during the score-breakdown animation described in GAME_FEEL.md ("the big number... rolls from 0 straight to the final score"), not a scoring bug — but worth a look if `/text` (or any external reader) can catch it mid-roll.

## 8. Top 5 recommendations (ranked) and grade

1. **Fix or telegraph the pre-chapter-7 transit dependency (Bug 1).** Either make M28 (transit) genuinely inactive until Chapter 7 (per the game's own §0 invariant), or move the transit tutorial earlier, or add a visible "no revenue: missing transit" tag (not just "Network short: 0 of 0.5") on any rack affected before that chapter unlocks. This single fix would likely have prevented Run 1's bankruptcy outright.
2. **Add a running "pace" signal** (e.g., a faint always-visible comparison line to what Greedy is doing on the same seed, or a quarter-over-quarter percentile), so the enormous human-vs-bot gap (§2) is felt mid-game, not just revealed at the final screen. Right now the game's best anti-slop feature (bot comparison) is also its biggest missed teaching opportunity, because it only fires once, at the end.
3. **Re-pace the first four chapters.** Spread Chapters 2–4 (power, GPUs/roofline, heat) out, or add a short "nothing new, just play" buffer chapter, so a first-time player has a full quarter to internalize drag-install and the cash gauge before three more systems land.
4. **Merge or gate Chapters 9/12/16** (memory market, energy sourcing, policy) behind an actual forced decision on first appearance, rather than a modal a player can permanently ignore — per DESIGN.md's own ablation data, these currently contribute the least measured depth.
5. **Reconcile DESIGN.md with the shipped game**, or add a one-line banner at its top pointing to SPEC.md as current — a grader or new contributor who reads DESIGN.md first (as this brief's instructions direct) will spend real time confused by a "6 chapters / 3 years" description of a 17-chapter, 5-year game.

**Letter grade: B.**
Justification: the core systems (§2) genuinely deliver Lecture 3's test — "the more you think, the better you do" is not a claim here, it's something I felt directly, twice, at two very different skill levels, with a score gap (60×–550×) large enough that no reasonable amount of noise explains it away. The juice (§3) is restrained and mostly information-carrying, and "make losing fun" (the bankrupt/finished postmortem) is the best-executed single feature in the build. What holds it back from an A is that the depth is currently *unfelt* for a good-faith, non-bankrupt, chapter-completing player — I did nearly everything the tutorial told me to and still landed in the "idle" tier of the game's own balance targets — combined with one concrete, reproducible bug (the pre-chapter transit trap) that turns "learn by losing" into "lose for reasons the game hasn't explained yet." Both are fixable without touching the underlying simulation; that's exactly why this sits at a B and not lower.
